# Kavian — A Public Infrastructure for Understanding, Oversight, and Collective Improvement

**Status, 22 September 2026.** This document is the service definition. It is not the slide script and not a running system. The path, the scene, Berlin, *Kāveh*, and the personal limits are in the [English proposal](../tnc-kavian-presentation/PROPOSAL-en.md) and in the [deck](../tnc-kavian-presentation/proposal-deck.html).

Kavian is a service that connects knowledge, people’s lived experience, human judgment, and artificial intelligence. It helps society understand public problems, examine the exercise of power, evaluate possible solutions, and follow the consequences of decisions over time.

Kavian is concerned with the relationship between **what happens in society**, **the decisions that shape it**, and **the lives of the people affected**.

Power, in this sense, may sit with a government, company, media organization, civic institution, or technological system. Wherever a decision affects collective life, people should be able to ask what evidence supports it, who is responsible, what its consequences are, and how it can be changed. Kavian itself must be open to the same questions.

Kavian grows from a commitment to freedom and better lives for the people of Iran. Its wider horizon is a way of creating public knowledge that can learn from different cultures and communities and be useful beyond Iran.

## 1. The problem Kavian addresses

Information, specialist knowledge, lived experience, and decision-making authority are often held in different places.

People experience the consequences of a decision without necessarily seeing the documents or processes behind it. Specialists understand parts of a problem but may miss the experience of those affected. Institutions produce reports, yet the connection between a promise, a decision, its cost, and its outcome can be difficult to trace. Public memory breaks as news cycles, leaders, and generations change.

Kavian connects these parts through an ongoing process:

**Understand the problem → gather evidence → hear different views → evaluate interpretations → develop options → act with care → examine the results.**

The aim is to make the ability to understand and follow public affairs available to more people, without making it depend on the continued presence of any one person, group, or authority.

## 2. The core unit: a living public dossier

A subject in Kavian can become a living dossier: a claim, news report, promise, decision, social problem, public program, or proposal for change.

A dossier does not end when a report is published. New evidence, criticism, institutional responses, and observed outcomes can be added, and earlier assessments can be revised.

Depending on the subject, a dossier asks:

- What happened, and how do we know?
- For whom is this a problem, and from whose perspective?
- Which decisions, rules, and interests shaped it?
- Who benefited, who bore the costs, and who has been overlooked?
- What interpretations and solutions exist, and where do they disagree?
- What action is possible, who is responsible for it, and how will its results be examined?

“Living” means that conclusions remain open to revision. Kavian can still state clearly what the available evidence supports today, while showing the limits of that conclusion.

## 3. Kavian’s conceptual architecture

Kavian brings together several connected capacities:

| Capacity | Role |
|---|---|
| Public participation and lived experience | Raise issues, express needs, provide experience and evidence, challenge accounts, and follow outcomes |
| Evidence and memory | Connect sources, claims, decisions, institutions, and consequences; preserve the history of changes |
| Artificial intelligence | Search, extract, compare, find connections, support early analysis, raise questions, and prepare accessible explanations |
| Network of advisers | Add knowledge, experience, criticism, and considered judgment wherever needed, without deciding for the public |
| Publication and paths to action | Present findings, options, proposals, and tools for public follow-up and advocacy |
| Governance and accountability | Set the rules and limits of authority, handle challenges, and oversee Kavian’s own conduct |

These capacities do not operate as a rigid, one-way pipeline. A local community’s criticism may change how a problem is framed. An economist’s assessment may send an inquiry back to the evidence-gathering stage. The outcome of a policy may challenge assumptions shared by both human reviewers and AI systems.

## 4. The network of advisers

“Network of advisers” is the working name for this layer. “Elites” could imply a hierarchy over the public, while “human evaluators” would reduce a broader role to final approval. Advisers are people with relevant knowledge, skill, or experience in their work or field: researchers, economists, sociologists, writers, literary figures, public intellectuals, technical specialists, civic participants, and others who can deepen understanding of a subject. The name recognizes competence without granting political superiority or authority to decide for people.

Its diversity must extend beyond professional disciplines. Differences in worldview, cultural background, social position, and experience matter too. Contrasting perspectives can help reveal hidden assumptions.

The network’s role is not confined to approving a final report. Kavian may draw on it at different stages:

- Has the right question been asked, or is a more important problem hidden behind it?
- Is the evidence sufficient for this claim?
- Is the analysis technically and logically sound?
- What human experience, meaning, or consequence has the system missed?
- Which solution could work, and under what conditions?
- What needs further investigation?

The appropriate mix of people is invited for each question. A person’s authority must be relevant to the question at hand. A writer’s understanding of language and public narratives is different from an economist’s ability to assess budgetary effects; both may be essential to understanding the same problem.

People directly affected by a subject must also have their own route into the process. Their experience should not depend on receiving an “expert” title to be heard.

### Artifacts and feedback

One intended path is this: news, documents, datasets, reports, and accounts from the public enter a living dossier. With human and computational assistance, Kavian turns them into reviewable artifacts. Here, an “artifact” is a bounded piece of work, not a final verdict: for example, a claim-and-evidence card, a timeline, a comparison of conflicting accounts, a list of open questions, or a description of options and possible consequences. These are illustrative design proposals; the exact formats have not been selected.

An artifact may go to one adviser or to several advisers with different fields of competence and perspectives. Routing should depend on the subject and the question, not merely on fame or closeness to the operators. Each adviser could challenge a claim, source, assumption, wording, or overlooked human consequence; introduce evidence; state the limits of their knowledge; or argue that the question itself has been framed incorrectly. Subject to safety and privacy constraints, feedback should remain connected to the artifact and the evidence it addresses.

Kavian then places the responses in relation: what is supported, challenged, or still unknown; whether a disagreement concerns data, interpretation, or values; and what needs further inquiry. A “complete view” must not imply omniscience. The aim is an honest, many-sided account of what is and is not known at present. Meaningful disagreement should not disappear into an average or an apparently neutral summary. Readers should be able to follow each position back to its reasoning and evidence.

This is not a one-way pipeline. Adviser feedback may revise an artifact or the question itself; affected people may challenge the advisers' synthesis; later evidence may change a published assessment. Advisers help the system understand. They are neither the final authority on truth nor representatives of the public.

## 5. Proposed methods for deeper and more legible judgment

One proposed way to work with this network is a process informed by Delphi. For contested public questions, a Policy Delphi approach may be more useful than a version aimed only at consensus: its purpose would be to clarify options and reasons for agreement and disagreement, not to force one view.

First, the question, evidence, and evaluation criteria are made clear. Participants give their assessments independently. They then receive a structured account of the arguments, points of agreement, and disagreements, and can reconsider their views. The outcome may be a shared assessment, several distinct positions, or an acknowledgment that the evidence is insufficient.

AI can help organize this process, but participants must be able to inspect its summaries. Omitting or misrepresenting an argument could change the result.

Other methods worth testing include structured expert elicitation to record common questions, assumptions, and uncertainty; multi-criteria decision analysis to compare options against explicit criteria such as cost, fairness, feasibility, and risk; and sensitivity analysis to show how changing criteria weights or assumptions changes a result. Naming these methods does not mean a formula has been chosen or implemented. The value-laden choice and weighting of criteria cannot be handed to advisers while excluding public participation.

Weights, where useful, should depend on the question. A starting proposal is to consider relevant expertise, directly applicable experience, and a reviewable record of past assessments. A conflict of interest may require limiting someone’s role or excluding them from a particular evaluation. Fame, proximity to the founder, or agreement with the majority should not create weight by themselves.

Three kinds of judgment must remain distinct:

- **The credibility of a factual claim** depends on evidence and the method used to examine it.
- **An expert estimate under uncertainty** may benefit from structured collective judgment and weighting.
- **A social or moral preference** belongs to public participation and choice; expert weights cannot replace it.

For example, specialists may estimate the likely effects of a policy. Whether people regard the distribution of its costs and benefits as fair is not solely a specialist question.

The output of this layer should therefore be more than a final score. It should show the reasons for an assessment, the degree of disagreement, its key assumptions, and whether changing the weights would change the result. The precise weighting method, selection of participants, and process for challenging an assessment still need to be designed and tested.

## 6. Technical layer and the place of models

In the architecture described by Kavian's founder for its development, the system is multi-agent: separate agents and processes may handle incoming material, create artifacts, route questions, combine feedback, and keep a record of each step. LangChain and LangGraph have been named for composing these flows, and LangSmith and Langfuse for observability and evaluation. He also reports that the system runs on Vercel, GitHub supports work on the code, and substantial observability work has been done. These are his reports about the technical layer. The present concept files do not independently establish its configuration, deployment, or performance.

Observability here is more than measuring latency and errors. It should be possible to understand which input and source version contributed to an output, which agent or model took part, what a person changed, where uncertainty or disagreement entered, and why a dossier or proposal was revised. That requirement sits in tension with protecting contributor identities, limiting access, and minimizing retained data; not every trace should be public. Exactly what the public, advisers, and independent overseers can see remains a design question.

The model layer should be replaceable and task-specific. Language models and, where appropriate, open models may help search, extract, translate, compare, and explain. A different ability is a small model that answers only a fixed question — one choice from a list, how likely a yes is, or a place on a scale — and returns a probability instead of a paragraph. Jev, from TypeSafe, is a hosted service with that ability. [Laya](https://huggingface.co/convaiinnovations/laya) is an open-weight model of the same kind, under Apache 2.0. The ability is what Kavian would use. Neither model writes the public account, and neither chooses what is true, fair, or worth doing. Claims about speed, cost, and calibration, from either maker, stay untested on Kavian's languages, data, and questions. A probability from either model is not “the probability that a news report is true” or “the best decision for people.”

LangChain and LangGraph are open-source; Langfuse's core is open-source and self-hostable, though some features are commercial; LangSmith's client SDK is open-source, but the LangSmith platform itself should not be described that way. Jev is a licensed, hosted service. Laya's weights are open, which is not the same thing as an audit of Kavian. Vercel and GitHub are external services. Thus “using open-source components,” “making Kavian auditable,” and “being independent of providers” are three different claims. Desired independence must be built and tested through architecture, replaceability, transparent decisions, and data governance, rather than inferred from a list of tools.

## 7. Kavian can analyze, propose, and advocate

Kavian can do more than preserve information. It can explain problems, develop options, make proposals, and advocate for accountability.

Its responsibility is to keep the boundaries between evidence, interpretation, and recommendation visible. A reader should be able to tell what was observed, what was inferred, and which proposal depends on particular values or assumptions.

If Kavian recommends or supports a public action, it must take responsibility for that position: its reasoning, the people responsible for it, its possible consequences, and the route for correcting it should be clear. Approval by its expert network does not, by itself, authorize Kavian to speak for everyone.

Success cannot be measured only by the number of reports produced or the attention they attract. Kavian must ask whether public understanding improved, participation became more possible, an error was corrected, harm was reduced, or an institution became more accountable.

## 8. Many fields, with a view toward the future

Kavian has a common foundation that can serve different fields: the economy, culture, education, health, the environment, technology, rights, public services, and emerging issues whose boundaries are not yet clear.

Each field needs appropriate sources, methods, and human contributors. Connections between fields are part of the service’s value: an economic decision may also have cultural, environmental, and social consequences.

Over time, Kavian could examine possible futures as well as past and present decisions. What may happen if a policy continues? Which alternatives have received too little attention? Who may bear its future costs?

Scenarios and simulations would serve as tools for inquiry and comparison. Their assumptions and uncertainties must remain visible.

## 9. The commitment connecting every part

Kavian is grounded in human dignity, freedom, meaningful participation, fairness, and a willingness to correct itself. Independence does not mean an absence of values. It means that no party, funder, founder, or intellectual circle can claim the service as its property.

The network of contributors, the people operating Kavian, and those overseeing Kavian itself must have distinct responsibilities. Expertise does not confer ownership of the public agenda. Operating the infrastructure must not create hidden authority to erase an issue or silence criticism.

Kavian should also record successes, effective repairs, and productive cooperation, so society can learn from what works. Its commitment to people is fulfilled when they gain more ability to understand their circumstances, make choices, work together, and change the conditions of their lives.
